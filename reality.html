THE WINNING FORMULA

The strongest single position is somewhat higher taxes on the rich (59%), stronger than "much higher" (57%). The largest penalty for a populist message goes to "overthrow the political elite" (40%). "Defend citizens' interests" wins (53%). Voters want the rich to pay more. They don't want a revolution. Reform beats rupture.

EXTREMES LOSE ON BOTH SIDES

On refugees, EU withdrawal (38%) and "admit a great many" (37%) are among the most punished positions on the board. Some kinds of moderation pay. On globalization voters barely care (47–51%), so it's not a battlefield. Spend your words where the bars are long.

WIRED INTO THE MODEL

In the democracy run, the Ownership Party's tax-the-rich platform and its message now shift popularity using these effects, discounted for real elections. Over 30 random histories, the "defend citizens" message governs ~70% of years. "Overthrow the elite" governs ~42%. The message is worth almost three decades of power.

THE NETWORK YOU SHARED

A co-membership map of international political economy research networks: IPES (International Political Economy Society, mostly US), IPEG (the UK's British International Studies Association group), CPERN (the European Sociological Association's critical political economy network), AIPEN (Australia) and PEIO (the political economy of international organizations conference). The core is the US, UK, Canada, Australia and Northwestern Europe. Most of the world sits on the edge, attached by a single thin line.

WHAT IT MEANS FOR YOU

The ideas that shape policy are made in a handful of rooms. You get into them the same way you got here: a working model, public data, honest falsifiers. The thin-line countries are the opening. Mexico, India, Kazakhstan and Egypt are under-studied, and that's where an outsider's work can be first rather than fifteenth.
